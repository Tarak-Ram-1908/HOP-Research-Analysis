Can a network promise that your data will arrive on time, every time?

For our Computer Networks (CS3001) research-paper analysis at IIITDM Kancheepuram, we studied HOP (Chiariotti et al., IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking, 2021). HOP is a multipath transport protocol that sends packet-level FEC across WiFi, LTE and mmWave paths so that each data block arrives within 35 ms with 98% reliability. That is the kind of guarantee AR and remote robotics need.

Reading the paper wasn't enough for us, so we wrote our own simulator in Python, rebuilt HOP's scheduler (JUMP) and capacity estimator (SKIP), and ran 2,300+ simulations.

What we found:

🔹 A formula subtlety. Implemented literally, one equation in the paper (eq. 5) reports a 0% failure chance as soon as any redundancy is added. Under the paper's own model the exact term is λ(d+1) − λ(d), and it matches Monte Carlo to within 0.1 percentage points. HOP clearly works in the authors' results, so this is most likely a documentation slip. But anyone re-implementing it from the paper would get a scheduler that is far too optimistic.

🔹 About 5,000× faster. The paper computes the failure probability by enumerating every delivery pattern, which grows exponentially with the number of paths. The same sum is a convolution, so it runs in polynomial time: 34 µs instead of 169 ms at 4 paths, with identical results. That makes HOP practical on phones with 3 to 6 interfaces.

🔹 Graceful degradation. When the 98% target becomes unreachable, HOP adds maximum redundancy, which builds queues and makes the next block late too. Falling back to the best reachable target breaks that loop: in one scenario it kept 35.2 Mb/s of on-time goodput where the original collapsed to 0.8 Mb/s.

🔹 Correlated paths. HOP assumes the paths fail independently. When they share a bottleneck, it promised 1.94% failures and delivered 2.86%. A one-factor correlation-aware model kept its promises calibrated.

My biggest takeaway: re-implementing a paper teaches you far more than reading it. The protocol's core idea holds up well, and the interesting questions were in the details.
